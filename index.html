Australia's Wildlife Crisis

The Silent Invasion

Since 1788, humans have released hundreds of foreign species across Australia. Most Australians have no idea how much has already been lost, or how much it has cost.

Australia is one of the most biologically extraordinary places on Earth. Around 80% of its mammals, reptiles, and flowering plants exist nowhere else in the world. Millions of years of isolation produced animals found in no other country, including the platypus, the quoll, the bilby, and the numbat.

Yet today, Australia holds a record no country wants: the worst modern mammal extinction rate on the planet. More mammal species have been lost here since European settlement than on any other continent.

The numbers below are not projections. They are the current reality.

The crisis is not evenly spread. Some states carry a disproportionate share of the burden, and the map below shows exactly where the pressure is greatest.

New South Wales carries the heaviest burden with 760 threatened species, nearly double the next closest state. The ACT, despite being tiny, records 71 threatened species within its borders. No state is untouched.

Part 2

The Culprits: A 200-Year Invasion

None of this happened overnight. The invasion began the moment the First Fleet arrived in 1788, and it has never stopped.

Each introduction seemed reasonable at the time. Cats for pest control. Rabbits for sport hunting. Foxes for recreation. Cane toads for agriculture. Every decision had a rationale. None had an exit plan.

Over two centuries, these choices compounded into a continental-scale ecological emergency. The timeline below captures how quickly the damage accumulated, and how little time it took for each species to spread beyond any hope of containment.

Of all the species introduced, the feral cat stands alone in the scale of its destruction. The next two maps show exactly how completely it has colonised the continent.

The dot size reflects how many native species each invader threatens. The European Rabbit displaced hundreds of native species through habitat destruction. The feral cat remains the single deadliest predator in Australian history.

Confirmed feral cat sightings show density along the east coast and throughout inland regions, reflecting decades of unchecked expansion.

Each species has carved out a different ecological niche. European rabbits dominate the arid interior and southern coasts, while cane toads remain concentrated in the north and east.

Part 3

The True Cost: What We Have Lost

Invasive species are often framed as a conservation issue. In Australia, they are also an economic burden of extraordinary scale. Damage to agriculture, grazing, forestry, and infrastructure has accumulated for decades, turning ecological disruption into a national financial problem.

But money is only the visible part of the story. The deeper loss is biological: species erased, ecosystems destabilised, and animal groups pushed into increasingly fragile ranges.

To understand the crisis, we need to read it in two ways at once: what invasion costs in dollars, and what it costs in life.

Economic burden

The bill is concentrated

Not every invasive species causes the same level of damage. A small group accounts for the largest recurring costs, and the feral cat sits at the top. That imbalance matters because it shows where management has the greatest potential return.

Feral cats cost more per year than any other tracked invasive species in Australia, with economic damage far exceeding most other major invaders.

Birds face the greatest total number of listed threats, but mammals have suffered the greatest extinction toll.

Economic losses are enormous, but extinction is irreversible. Once a species disappears, the damage cannot be recovered through funding, policy, or restoration. The next chart shifts the story from annual cost to permanent loss.

Ecological loss

Extinction is not evenly shared

The extinction record is not random. Certain invasive predators have repeatedly driven native mammals beyond recovery, and the pattern reveals how vulnerable Australia’s fauna has been to introduced threats.

The chart does not show abstract numbers. Each circle represents a species that no longer exists.

Extinction is the endpoint, but threat begins long before that. Across Australia, invasive species are pressuring different animal classes in different ways, and that pressure is not evenly distributed across states.

This diagram connects invasive species to the native animal classes they threaten. Feral cats and European rabbits reach across the entire system, touching every major class. The crisis is not caused by one invader alone, but a few species dominate the network of harm.

Reading across the rows reveals which animal classes are most at risk in each state. New South Wales dominates much of the matrix, showing how concentrated threat has become. The burden is national, but its intensity is highly uneven across geography and species groups.

By this point, the pattern is unmistakable. The invasion is expensive, selective, and systemic. A relatively small set of introduced species is responsible for a disproportionate share of Australia’s ecological and economic damage.

Part 4

Fighting Back: Can Australia Win?

The story of invasive species in Australia is not only one of loss. It is also one of hard-won, painstaking progress.

Island eradication programs have demonstrated that when resources are concentrated and the geography is contained, complete eradication is achievable.

The harder question is whether that model can scale. The mainland is not an island. Reinvasion is constant. Budgets are finite. Political will is inconsistent.

Given finite resources, the question is no longer just what is happening, but which species should Australia act on first.

Species in the top-left zone cause the most damage but receive the least management attention. These are Australia's highest priority targets. The feral cat sits furthest into the danger zone: highest cost, lowest coverage, intractable at continental scale. Fire ant is the one bright spot, with high cost but already under coordinated eradication effort.

Island programs reached complete eradication, while mainland programs show progress but not total success.

The ACT and Victoria lead on management coverage relative to their size, while Western Australia and the Northern Territory lag behind.

The work is not finished.

Australia has proven that eradication is possible on islands. The next challenge is scaling that success to a continent. Every dollar invested in invasive species management returns multiple dollars in avoided ecological and economic damage. The silent invasion can be slowed, but only if Australia chooses to act at the scale the problem demands.
