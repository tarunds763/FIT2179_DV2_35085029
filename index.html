Australia's Wildlife Crisis

The Silent Invasion

Since 1788, humans have unleashed hundreds of foreign species across Australia. The damage is greater than most Australians will ever know.

Part 1

A Paradise with a Problem

Australia is one of the most biologically extraordinary places on Earth. Around 80% of its mammals, reptiles, and flowering plants exist nowhere else in the world. Nowhere else will you find a platypus, a quoll, or a bilby. This continent evolved in isolation for 50 million years — producing life unlike anything seen elsewhere.

Yet today, Australia holds a record no country wants — the worst modern mammal extinction rate on the planet. Since European settlement, more Australian mammals have vanished forever than on any other continent. The question is not whether Australia is losing its wildlife. The question is why.

Part 2

The Culprits: A 200-Year Invasion

None of this happened overnight. The invasion of Australia's ecosystems began the moment the First Fleet arrived in 1788 — and it has never stopped. Cats came as pets. Rabbits were released for sport hunting. Foxes followed for the thrill of the chase. Cane toads were deliberately introduced in 1935 to control beetles on sugar cane farms. Nearly every introduction was intentional. Nearly every one became a disaster.

Over two centuries, these decisions compounded into a continental-scale ecological emergency that most Australians still don't fully grasp.

Of all the invaders, none has been more devastating than the feral cat. Originally brought as a domestic companion, it has spread to every corner of the continent — from the tropical north to the arid centre to the cool south. There is no region of Australia the feral cat has not reached.

And cats are not alone. Four species in particular have blanketed the continent in their own way — each arriving for a different reason, each now impossible to remove.

The spread is staggering. But what does it actually cost — in animals lost, and in dollars spent?

Part 3

The True Cost: What We Have Lost

Most Australians think of invasive species as an environmental issue — a concern for scientists and conservationists. In reality, it is also one of the most expensive problems this country faces. Billions of dollars in agricultural damage, billions more in failed eradication programs, and an ecological debt that simply cannot be repaid.

Behind every dollar figure is a species pushed closer to the edge. The feral cat alone is implicated in the extinction of at least 20 native mammal species and threatens hundreds more. This is not a distant, abstract crisis — it is happening in national parks, in backyards, and in the bush outside every Australian city right now.

When ranked by total economic damage, the scale becomes visceral. Feral cats top the list — not because they are the most expensive to manage, but because the ecological destruction they cause translates directly into losses for farmers, land managers, and governments alike.

The ecological damage follows a clear pattern. Invasive predators — cats and foxes — overwhelmingly threaten ground-dwelling mammals and nesting birds. Herbivores like rabbits and goats destroy the vegetation that every other species depends on. Each invader carves out its own trail of destruction.

Invasive species are not just one threat among many. Across every class of Australian animal, they rank as the single greatest driver of decline — ahead of habitat clearing, ahead of drought, ahead of fire.

The damage is not uniform across the country. Some states face a far more concentrated set of threats than others — and some invasive species are far more destructive in certain regions than they are elsewhere.

Australia is not standing still. Over the past four decades, the country has launched some of the most ambitious pest control programs in the world. Some have worked. Others show just how hard this fight really is.

Part 4

Fighting Back: Can Australia Win?

The story of invasive species in Australia is not only one of loss. It is also one of hard-won, painstaking progress. Myxomatosis cut rabbit populations by 90% within years of its release. Island eradication programs have given dozens of native species a second chance. The bilby, once thought headed for extinction, now breeds in fenced sanctuaries across three states.

But for every success, the scale of what remains is sobering. Feral cats alone occupy 99.8% of the Australian continent. No single program, no matter how well funded, has come close to reversing that.

Active management programs are concentrated in the south-east and in island territories — where populations are smaller and eradication is actually achievable. The vast interior remains largely unmanaged.

Australia's wildlife crisis is not inevitable — but it is urgent. Every year of inaction means more species lost, more dollars spent on damage that could have been prevented, and more ground conceded to animals that were never meant to be here.

The data is clear. The science is settled. What remains is the will to act — and the awareness that this is everyone's problem, not just a concern for specialists and conservationists. Australia's wildlife belongs to all of us. So does the responsibility to protect it.
